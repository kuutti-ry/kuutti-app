# ADR-045: The app lock

- Status: accepted 2026-10-22 (#418's lock half, built as written here on the maintainer's answers of that day; a native module, so it ships with #184's native batch or before it, §8; the points under "Open, the maintainer's" are his to confirm or change)
- Date: 2026-10-22
- Follows: TD-1 (every account is a bank login; there is no password to reset), TD-2 (Expo and EAS as the native shell), ADR-016 (a fresh bank login every time), ADR-027 (§1: the keychain's class for what guards a session; §5: the random source and its `shortcut:`), ADR-032 (the notices; the push is #102's), ADR-043 (the e-mail, after which the lock is offered), ADR-021 (tests at every level), CLAUDE.md "Decisions" (a change of identity carries an ADR), "Security and privacy defaults" (a new dependency) and Accessibility; issues #418, #184, #296, #102

## Context

Nothing locked the app. Whoever holds an unlocked phone opens Kuutti signed in: the matches, the conversations and the photos sent in them, whom the person seeks. The session lives in the keychain or keystore as `WHEN_UNLOCKED_THIS_DEVICE_ONLY` (`apps/mobile/src/lib/session.ts`), so the phone's own lock was its only guard. The person who picks up an unlocked phone is most often somebody close: a partner, a family member, a friend handed the phone to look at a picture.

#418 asked for a lock the person may turn on: the phone's face or fingerprint, or a passcode of Kuutti's own, off until then. The maintainer answered its questions on 22/10/2026. The lock ships now, with its native module. It gates the screens only (#418's proposal 1, (a)). Biometrics never fall back to the phone's own code (2). Android's weak biometrics count (3). The passcode is six digits, and five wrong ones sign the phone out (4). The time in the background before it locks again is the person's choice of straight away, 1, 5 or 15 minutes, 1 by default (5; he wrote "1-15?", read as that range). What a push notice says while the lock is on is left out of this issue (6).

## Decision

1. **The lock gates the screens, not the session.** The threat is a person with the phone in hand, for a minute. While the app is locked, the lock screen lies over everything (`LockProvider`, `apps/mobile/src/features/identity/lock/`): on iOS in a view of the window's own (react-native-screens' `FullWindowOverlay`, as the dialogs are), which nothing presented can refuse; on Android in a modal, a window of its own. The screens under it, the session and the chat's socket go on as before; nothing of them shows through.
   - **Why not the session's own item** (#418's (b), `requireAuthentication` in expo-secure-store). The keychain would ask for the face or finger before it hands out the token. But a new enrolment invalidates the item, so every new face or finger on the phone would mean a bank login. It cannot work with a passcode of our own. And nothing in the background, the token's refresh included, could read the item. The threat is a hand, not a forensic tool, and the screens are what a hand reaches.

2. **On the phone only, and bound to the session.** The lock is two items in SecureStore: `kuutti.lock` (whether the face or finger opens it, the passcode's hash, the time in the background) and `kuutti.lock.tries` (the wrong passcodes in a row), both `WHEN_UNLOCKED_THIS_DEVICE_ONLY`, as the session they guard: on this phone only, readable while it is unlocked, never in a backup. Each is written in place (`lock/store.ts`): the session's items are deleted before each write, since the keychain keeps an item's first class on an update and some predate the class; only this code ever wrote the lock's, with this class, and a write that fails then leaves the lock and its count as they were, never off. Nothing of the lock reaches the API: no route, no column, no event, no line of the export, so erasure and the export do not change (ADR-007). The web preview has no lock.
   - **Any sign-out turns it off.** Whenever the app has no session, the stored lock and its tries are removed: Log out, Log out everywhere from another phone, a break, a sanction, an erasure, the refresh refused, and the lock's own way out (§6). A sign-in starts unlocked, with the lock off, and the person turns it on again. A lock that outlived its session would lock the next person on a shared phone out with somebody else's passcode, and the bank login has already said who is signing in.

3. **The passcode.** Six digits (`PASSCODE_LENGTH`), typed into one field that hides them, on the number pad. The field tells the phone nothing about a password or a one-time code, so autofill neither offers nor keeps it. A new passcode is typed twice; two that differ start again, said in words.
   - **Stored as a salted hash, stretched by nothing.** `{ salt, hash }`: the salt is a native version-4 UUID without its dashes, the hash the SHA-256 of the salt, a colon and the digits (`@noble/hashes`, already pinned), compared in constant time (`lock/passcode.ts`). Six digits are a million codes. A machine tries a million SHA-256 hashes in well under a second, and stretching cannot change that at a price a phone pays on every unlock: PBKDF2-SHA256 at 10,000 rounds took about 0.8 s on this Mac's V8 without its JIT, the class of engine Hermes is, so a phone would wait seconds for each unlock, while a graphics card still tries the million in seconds. What guards the passcode is where the hash lies, this phone's keychain while it is unlocked (§2), and the five tries. Whoever can read that keychain holds the session's tokens from it too, so the passcode keeps nothing more from them. The hash is there so that the digits themselves are never in the store, since people reuse the code of their phone or their bank. The salt has to be unique, not secret: it uses ADR-027 §5's random source, with the same ceiling, and `expo-crypto` at #184 lifts both.
   - **Five tries, counted before they are checked.** Each try is written to `kuutti.lock.tries` before the digits are compared, so closing the app between the typing and the answer gives no try back, and the count is kept across restarts. The right passcode, or the face or finger, sets it back to none. A wrong one says how many tries are left, in words: an assertive live region, said aloud on iOS, and nothing shakes. The fifth wrong one signs this phone out as §6 does, and the sign-in screen says "Signed out by the lock". A store that cannot count still checks the digits, and the right ones open. Tries already spent are never checked again: a lock that shows with five on the count, or a sixth try, goes to the bank at once, so a sign-out cut short and a restart buy no more guesses. The sign-out waits for the API five seconds at most (§6).

4. **When it locks.** While the person is signed in and the lock is on:
   - **At a cold start.** The first moment the app is signed in with the lock read, it is locked. Until the lock is read, a signed-in app is covered.
   - **After the grace time in the background.** Going to the background records the time. Coming back after at least the person's choice, straight away, 1, 5 or 15 minutes (`GRACE_MINUTES`, 1 by default), it locks. The time away is the longer of the wall clock's and of a clock nobody sets (`performance.now`), and a wall clock set back to before the departure counts as long enough; so a clock set back while the phone is awake is caught. `shortcut:` a wall clock set back to a time after the departure while the phone sleeps is not caught, since that steady clock stops in sleep; a native read of the elapsed time since boot (Android's `elapsedRealtime`, iOS's `mach_continuous_time`) in a native batch lifts it (Open 7). On Android "straight away" also locks after the app's own photo picker, share sheet or a permission dialog, each an activity of its own, which iOS presents inside the app. The grace is a setting of the person's phone, not a matching rule, so it is not in `matching_config` (#418's proposal 5).
   - **A cover for the app switcher.** The app's name on its own background, hidden from screen readers, is drawn over the screens when iOS goes `inactive` (the app switcher opening, a call, Control Centre pulled down) or to the background, and when Android's window loses its focus (`blur`: recents opening, the notification shade) or goes to the background. It goes when the app is active again, or when Android's window has the focus back; nothing locks for it alone. What the phone's own face or finger prompt does to the app's state is ignored, since the prompt itself makes iOS inactive; but a real trip to the background while it is up is kept, and judged once it answers: away still, the cover is drawn and the return decides; back already, the time away decides at once; a yes forgives it, since the owner's face or finger was there.
   - **At a cold start the phone is asked first.** While the phone has not yet said whether it still has the face or finger the lock was set with, the cover stands, so the lock screen comes with the right ways in and asks once. The phone is asked again each time the app comes back, as a face or finger may have been added or removed meanwhile.
   - **The cover is a view; the lock is not.** The cover lies over the screens as a plain view, not as a modal: on Android a modal is a window of its own, which takes the focus from the app's window, and that loss is what draws the cover, so a modal cover would stand for good once the shade closed. The lock's own Android modal takes the focus the same way and draws the cover under it, where it shows nothing; opening the lock clears it. On iOS the lock is no modal at all: a modal is presented by the app's root view controller, which refuses while a photo picker or a share sheet is presented on it, and the refused lock would not come back until a cold start. The lock lies in a view of the window's own instead, above whatever is presented.
   - **`shortcut:` the cover is drawn from JavaScript.** It is drawn after the event reaches JavaScript, so it races the snapshot. On iOS the snapshot is taken as the app enters the background, after `inactive`, which usually leaves the cover time to draw. On Android the recents thumbnail may be taken before the cover is drawn, and then shows the last screen. `FLAG_SECURE` would hide it, but it also blocks screenshots, and #296 decided that screenshots are never blocked: a described screenshot is how a blind person reads a photo. `Activity.setRecentsScreenshotEnabled(false)` (Android 13 and later), behind a small native module of our own, would lift it (Open 3).
   - **A lock that cannot be read stays locked.** A store that fails, or an item in a shape this app never wrote, keeps the app locked with the bank as the only way out, rather than open (`readLock`).
   - **A known limit: open dialogs and sheets.** The app's dialogs are portalled above the screens, so one that is open lies above the cover too, and the snapshot shows it; the lock lies above them. On iOS a photo picker or a share sheet presented when the app went away lies above the cover in the snapshot as well.

5. **The phone's face or fingerprint.** Face ID or Touch ID on iOS, BiometricPrompt's face or fingerprint on Android, through `expo-local-authentication` (`lock/biometrics.ts`).
   - **No fallback to the phone's own code** (`disableDeviceFallback`, and an empty `fallbackLabel` that hides iOS's "Enter Password"). Somebody who knows the phone's code, often a partner, would open Kuutti with it. After the face or finger come Kuutti's passcode, if one is set, and then the bank.
   - **Android's weak class counts** (`biometricsSecurityLevel: "weak"`). Where a phone's face unlock is class 2, `strong` would not offer it, so "face" would quietly mean the fingerprint only. Which phones those are, nothing official says. What was found (#464): the Pixel 7's face unlocks only the phone itself, banking apps and sign-ins taking the fingerprint ([9to5Google](https://9to5google.com/2023/10/04/pixel-8-face-unlock/)), which is how a class 1 face behaves, class 1 being never offered to an app's prompt ([source.android.com](https://source.android.com/docs/security/features/biometric)), though the article names no class; Google says the Pixel 8's meets "the highest Android biometric standard" (the same article); and only second-hand reports put Samsung's below class 3. So `weak` changes anything only where a face is class 2. Weak is enough for a lock that guards the screens (§1).
   - **The kind the words name** is, on Android, the hardware the phone declares (the module reads the package manager's features, `android.hardware.biometrics.face` and Samsung's own among them), not what is enrolled or what the prompt takes. A phone with face hardware whose face is class 1, or with only a finger enrolled, reads "face or fingerprint" while its prompt takes the finger alone: the button and the switch then name a face the prompt will not take. That is the ceiling (`shortcut:` in `lock/biometrics.ts`). What the lock says after a try is worded around it: the "either" refusal and pause lines say "face or fingerprint", never "and", and claim nothing about which was tried. A phone whose only face is class 1 counts as having none, since `isEnrolledAsync` asks for the weak class, so the line for a phone without any says "no face or fingerprint unlock that Kuutti can use", which also holds for an iPhone whose Face ID Kuutti was refused. Android 12's `BiometricManager.Strings` gives the phone's own words for its prompt; the module does not expose it, so it waits for a native module in a native batch.
   - **Never the only way in.** The lock screen always offers the bank (§6), and the passcode when one is set. A phone whose face or finger was removed since shows no button for it.
   - **Asked once at the lock, and once when turned on.** The lock screen asks as it shows, if the person chose the face or finger; after that its button asks again. A cancel says nothing; a refusal says so in words, whole sentences in the kind the button names, since Finnish agrees with it. The phone's pause after too many tries (`lockout`: the try that brings it ends the prompt, and every ask during it answers with no prompt) is said as that, with the way it ends, never as a try again (#464). Android's compatibility definition recommends a pause of at least 30 seconds after five false tries and requires the phone's own code after at most twenty, and only that code may reset a pause ([CDD §7.3.10](https://source.android.com/docs/compatibility/16/android-16-cdd), Android 16, C-SR-5, C-1-9, C-7-2). iOS's lasts until the phone's passcode ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/localauthentication/laerror-swift.struct/code/biometrylockout)). The module gives one code for all of them, so each kind's sentence names the way that ends every pause, on both platforms: lock the phone and open it with its own code. Waiting is left out, since it never ends iOS's. Turning it on in Settings asks once, and anything but a yes leaves it off, said in words, the pause in its own.

6. **The way out is the bank.** "Sign in with the bank instead" is always on the lock screen, with a line that says what it does. It drops this phone's session as Log out does (the API's logout, waited for five seconds at most, then the tokens deleted), which turns the lock off (§2), and the sign-in screen says why ("Signed out by the lock"). The person signs in again with the bank (ADR-016). A forgotten passcode, a face that does not work, or five wrong tries all end there. So the lock needs no recovery of its own, nothing on the account changes, and nothing about the lock reaches the API.

7. **Off by default, offered once, and in Settings.** The lock is off until the person turns it on. It is offered once on this phone, among the later screens after the e-mail (`/account/lock`, "Lock Kuutti on this phone?"), with the same controls as Settings and a plain Not now, which becomes Done once something is on. Being shown is being offered: the phone remembers it (the preference `lockOffered`, kept across sign-outs) and never offers it again, nor on the web preview, nor while the lock is on. Settings has a Lock row after This device, signed in and outside the web preview, with On or Off as its value (`/settings/lock`): the face or finger as a switch, or the line that the phone has none Kuutti can use, both asked of the phone as Settings or the offer opens and again each time the app comes back from the background, lock on or off (#464; not after the phone's own prompt, which passes through iOS's inactive alone); the passcode set, changed or removed; while the lock is on, the four grace times as a radio group, and the line that Kuutti always locks when it starts. With neither the face nor a passcode, the lock is off and removed.

8. **The native module.** `expo-local-authentication` `~57.0.3`: MIT, Expo's own, in the SDK's range, no install script. Its config plugin adds Android's `USE_BIOMETRIC` and `USE_FINGERPRINT` permissions and iOS's Face ID purpose (`NSFaceIDUsageDescription`), which iOS needs before it shows Face ID. The plugin's default is English only, so the purpose comes from `messages.yaml`'s `native.ios.NSFaceIDUsageDescription` in three languages: the i18n build writes `packages/i18n/src/generated/native.json` from the keys `native.<platform>.<Key>`, refusing a key of another shape and a double quote or a backslash in a text, and `app.config.ts` puts the English in Info.plist and every language in `locales`. `CFBundleAllowMixedLocalizations` in `app.json` lets iOS show it in the phone's language. Native strings are new for this app. All of this moves the native fingerprint of both platforms: a native preview, new builds, and every installed build and the shared simulators' dev clients orphaned. So the change lands with #184's native batch or right before it, as one move of the fingerprint, not one more ahead of it.

## Consequences

- A person who turns the lock on meets it at every cold start and after the grace time, and the bank is always a way past it. A forgotten passcode costs a bank login and loses nothing.
- On a shared phone, whoever signs in next starts with no lock (§2), and the offer is not made again, since it is the phone's (§7). Settings has the lock.
- The app runs under the lock: a message or a match can arrive while it is locked, and shows after it opens. The chat's socket stays connected as before.
- The cover also shows while iOS's Control Centre or a call lies over the app, and while Android's notification shade is pulled down.
- Changing or removing the lock asks for nothing first: whoever can reach Settings is past the lock already (Open 2).
- Tests: jest-expo with the module mocked (`jest.setup.js`), and the specification in `features/identity/app-lock.feature`. Maestro has no biometric command, so the face and the finger are checked by hand on phones after the native build is installed (`docs/runbooks/devices.md`, the lock).
- No database, API or export change, and no migration.

### What it does not close

- Somebody who knows the phone's code can add their own face or finger to the phone, and the phone's prompt then accepts it: the module does not say that the enrolled set changed. A person who fears that leaves the face or finger off and uses the passcode alone (Open 6).
- Somebody who watches the passcode being typed.
- A reader of the phone's keychain finds the passcode by trying the million hashes, and holds the session's tokens from the same keychain anyway (§3).
- On Android, the recents thumbnail taken before the cover is drawn (§4).
- A dialog or, on iOS, a picker or share sheet left open: above the cover in the snapshot (§4).
- A wall clock set back while the phone sleeps, to a time after it went away (§4).
- A push notice shows on the phone's own lock screen whatever its text says; since #102 (ADR-055) its words name nobody and nothing (Open 1).
- Screenshots are not blocked (#296).

## Alternatives considered

- **The session's item behind the biometric** (`requireAuthentication`, #418's (b)): stronger against a forensic read, but every new face or finger means a bank login, it cannot work with a passcode of our own, and the refresh could not run in the background (§1).
- **The phone's code as a fallback** (the module's default): the partner who knows it opens Kuutti too (§5).
- **Android's strong class only:** a class 2 face, where a phone has one, would quietly not be offered (§5).
- **A stretched hash** (PBKDF2, scrypt, Argon2): seconds at every unlock on a phone, and a million codes are still tried in seconds elsewhere (§3).
- **A delay between wrong tries instead of a sign-out:** the person waits, and still needs a way out; the bank costs a minute and loses nothing.
- **A lock on the server** (a column, a route, a line of the export): it would need a recovery, an export line and an erasure line, and would follow the person to a new phone where no hand holds it.
- **`FLAG_SECURE` for the app switcher:** it blocks screenshots too, against #296 (§4).
- **A fixed grace time** (#418's proposal 5): the maintainer chose the person's choice.
- **The grace in `matching_config`:** it is no matching rule, and it is the person's, on their phone.
- **Keeping the lock across a sign-out:** the next person on a shared phone would meet somebody else's lock (§2).

## Open, the maintainer's

1. What a push notice says while the lock is on (#418's proposal 6, left out): #102 shows the same words with the lock on or off, since they name nobody and nothing and the lock never reaches the API (ADR-055 Open 3); whether it should say less is still the maintainer's.
2. Whether changing or removing the lock, or turning the face or finger off, asks for the passcode or the face first.
3. The Android recents ceiling (§4): a small native module with `setRecentsScreenshotEnabled(false)` for Android 13 and later, in a native batch, or accepted as it is.
4. The face and the finger checked by hand on devices (Face ID, Touch ID, Android's face and fingerprint), `docs/runbooks/devices.md`: nobody has run the checks yet; they wait for the native build.
5. The Finnish and Swedish words, the Face ID purpose among them: machine text until the native review.
6. Whether a new face or finger enrolled on the phone should turn the lock's biometrics off (What it does not close). That needs a native look at the enrolled set, such as iOS's `evaluatedPolicyDomainState`, which the module does not give.
7. A native clock of the time since boot for the grace time (§4's `shortcut:`), in a native batch, or accepted as it is.
